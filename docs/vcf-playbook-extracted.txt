1. Purpose and How to Use This Document

This playbook does two jobs at once. It explains what a VMware Cloud Foundation design actually consists of, and it converts that design into a sequenced program of work that a customer can use to consume a large adoption services entitlement inside a fixed twelve month window.

The fund in scope buys VCF adoption services only. It does not buy hardware and it does not buy licensing. That single constraint shapes every recommendation in this document, and it inverts the usual scoping instinct. When a fund can buy hardware, the fastest way to consume it is to build something new. When a fund can only buy services, the only way to consume it is depth: how far into each component the customer is willing to go, and how much of their team&apos;s time they can commit to absorbing it.

Section 8 is therefore the heart of this document rather than a late sub-decision. Sections 4 through 7 narrow the target; Section 8 determines whether the fund is spent.

Use it in this order:


Run the discovery inputs in Section 11 before proposing anything.


Work Sections 4 through 7 as a decision register with the customer. Record the choice and the reason, not just the choice.


Use Section 8 to set the depth tier for each component the customer selected. With a services-only fund, this is the primary drawdown mechanism, not a refinement.


Build the quarterly plan from Section 9 and price it against the levers in Section 10.



A note on sequencing

With a services-only fund the binding constraint is rarely the October 2027 date and never the hardware lead time. It is how much change the customer&apos;s platform team can absorb while still running production. Build the plan backward from their capacity and their change freeze calendar, then check it against the deadline.



2. The Forcing Function and the Funding Constraint


2.1 The deadline

vSphere 8 reaches End of General Support on 11 October 2027. That covers ESXi 8.x, vCenter Server 8.x, and vSAN 8.x. End of Technical Guidance follows on 11 October 2029, but technical guidance is documentation-based self-help with no patches, so it should not be treated as extra safe running time.

Two details matter in scoping conversations and are routinely missed:


Individual update releases have their own support windows. An older 8.0 update can fall out of support well before the line-level date. Inventory exact build numbers, not just major versions.


vSphere 8 is the last release available under perpetual licensing. What follows requires subscription, which is why the EOGS date functions as a whole-platform decision deadline rather than a routine upgrade.

Against that date, a twelve month program starting now lands with roughly a year of margin. That margin is the single strongest argument for starting while the entitlement is available rather than deferring to the final quarters, when professional services capacity across the market will be constrained and the fund will have expired.

Three consequences follow, and all three should be stated to the customer in the first meeting rather than discovered in month seven.


Pathway choice narrows. Any option requiring new hosts depends on a capital purchase the fund cannot make. Pathways that reuse existing hardware move to the front by default, not because they are technically superior but because they are the ones the fund can actually deliver.


Depth replaces breadth as the drawdown lever. A services fund is consumed by taking fewer components further, not by deploying more components shallowly. Section 8 is where that is decided.


Customer team capacity becomes the ceiling. Services cannot be delivered into an organisation that has nobody to receive them. This is the most common reason a large services entitlement expires partly unspent.




2.3 The delivery model

In these engagements CDW sells the VCF product, the customer receives adoption services entitlement against that purchase, and Broadcom Professional Services is the prime on the resulting services work. CDW delivers as subcontractor to Broadcom. The practical result is margin on the product sale and margin on the delivery, with the services attaching to the account team as well.

That structure is commercially attractive and operationally constraining at the same time, and the constraints are the part that gets underestimated.

4.2 Choosing between them

With a services-only entitlement the default changes. Converge an existing healthy vSphere cluster into the VCF management domain, then import the remaining production vCenters as workload domains in waves. Both pathways reuse hardware the customer already owns, both are services intensive, and both have lower host thresholds than a greenfield build. That combination is the best available fit for a fund that can pay for effort but not for equipment.

Reserve in-place upgrade for estates where the hardware is current, on the 9.x HCL, and reasonably uniform. It is the least services intensive pathway, which in this context is a weakness rather than a virtue, but it is the right answer where it is the right answer. Do not manufacture complexity to absorb budget.

Treat greenfield as conditional. If the customer already has approved capital for a refresh, it becomes viable and the services wrapped around it are substantial. If they do not, it is not a pathway, and proposing it wastes a discovery cycle.

5. Decision Two: Domain Topology

A VCF domain is a group of ESX hosts managed by one vCenter instance. The mapping is one vCenter per domain, and it is fixed. This is the single most common point of confusion in early design conversations.

Standard (separated)

Structure

Management and customer workloads share the management domain. One vCenter.

Management domain plus one or more VI workload domains. A vCenter per domain.

Isolation

vSphere resource pools separate management from workload VMs.

Hard separation. Management plane is unaffected by workload behaviour.

Entry host count

Lower. 3 or 4 depending on storage.

Higher. Management domain plus the first workload domain, roughly 7 or more.

Component placement

First VI workload domain triggers deployment of a vCenter instance and a three node NSX Manager cluster in the management domain.

Each subsequent workload domain adds another vCenter.

Recommended for

Small scale, proof of concept, environments intended to extend later.

Production at any meaningful scale. This is the recommended model.

Can you change later

Yes. Create a VI workload domain, move workload VMs out of the compute resource pool, then update shares and reservations on the pool left behind.

n/a



Standard architecture is almost always the right answer for a production estate. Consolidated exists to lower the entry host count, which matters when hardware is the constraint. Here it usually is not, because a customer converging an existing estate already owns the hosts and is redistributing them rather than buying more. Check that assumption during discovery: if the existing estate is genuinely tight on capacity, standard architecture may require hosts the entitlement cannot buy, and that becomes a capital conversation the customer has to open separately.

The exception is a customer running multiple small sites, where consolidated per site may be correct. There the services are consumed across site count rather than depth at any one site.




5.1 Workload domain segmentation

Once standard architecture is chosen, the next question is how many workload domains and on what boundary. Common and defensible boundaries:


Security or compliance zone, where an audit boundary makes a shared vCenter untenable.


Lifecycle independence, so that a domain running a legacy application can stay on an older version while the rest moves forward.


Tenancy, where business units require genuine administrative separation rather than RBAC within one vCenter.


Workload class, most commonly separating a VKS and AI domain from traditional VM workloads because the hardware profile genuinely differs.

Resist segmenting on geography alone when sites are well connected, and resist segmenting per application. Both produce vCenter sprawl that the customer pays to operate for years.

8. Decision Five: How Deep to Go in Each Component

Deploying a component and adopting it are different things, and the gap between them is where most entitlement funds are either well spent or quietly wasted. For each selected component, agree a depth tier explicitly. Tier 1 is deployed and functional. Tier 2 is genuinely in use by the platform team. Tier 3 changes how the business consumes infrastructure.

8.4 Efficiency features: memory tiering and vSAN

These are the easiest items to justify in the current market and should be near the front of any twelve month plan, because they change the hardware quantity in the rest of the plan.




NVMe memory tiering. Broadcom positions this as supporting materially higher VM density on existing hardware and improved TCO. With DRAM pricing where it is, this is the single most credible cost argument available. It also reduces the host count needed for every later phase, so run it early.


vSAN efficiency. Global deduplication and compression, QLC support, and the ability to re-use qualified legacy servers. For a customer with an array refresh due, model vSAN against the array renewal rather than against the VCF licence.


Topology aware scheduling and CPU tuning. Real gains on high core count modern processors. Low effort, usually a configuration and validation exercise rather than a project.

Treat these as a distinct early phase with measurable before and after numbers. They produce the evidence that funds the rest of the program internally.

9. The Twelve Month Drawdown Plan

This is a template, not a prescription. It assumes an existing estate being converged and imported rather than rebuilt, a standard architecture target, and a services-only entitlement. Compress or extend each phase against the customer&apos;s change calendar and team capacity.

The shape differs from a capital funded program in one important way. There is no large early commitment to anchor the spend, so the fund is consumed through sustained parallel effort across the full twelve months. That means the plan has to be staffed from week one and has to run several workstreams at once, which in turn means the customer needs more than one person available to receive it.

Because we deliver as subcontractor to the prime, the entire twelve months has to be scoped before the statement of work is signed. Treat the quarterly plan below as an input to that scoping exercise rather than something that evolves during delivery. Anything not named at SOW time will require a change order we do not control, and change orders across a partner boundary are slow enough that a late addition in Q3 frequently does not land inside the entitlement window at all.

The failure mode specific to a services-only fund

Capital programs fail by spending everything early and starving adoption. Services programs fail the opposite way: the engagement runs at the pace the customer&apos;s team can comfortably absorb, that pace turns out to be roughly half what the fund assumed, and a large balance expires. Model the required monthly burn rate in month one and review it monthly against actual. If the burn rate is below plan by the end of Q1, the plan is wrong and needs more parallel workstreams, not more time.




9.1 Burn rate and absorption

Divide the entitlement by twelve and ask whether the customer can genuinely receive that much delivery every month. Then check it against three limits.


Receiving capacity. Each workstream needs a named customer counterpart who can make decisions and attend sessions. A platform team of three cannot absorb five parallel workstreams regardless of how the fund is structured.


Change windows. Migration and upgrade work is gated by the change calendar. Enablement, design, policy authoring, and dashboard work are not. Load the non-disruptive workstreams into the freeze periods deliberately.


Delivery capacity. Confirm our own consultant availability across the full twelve months before committing to a burn rate. Booking the work is what protects it.



Where absorption is genuinely the constraint, the right answer is to shift scope toward workstreams that consume services without consuming much customer time: policy authoring, runbook development, dashboard and report construction, migration execution, and assessment work. These still deliver value and still draw the fund down, but they place less load on the customer&apos;s calendar than workshop-led adoption does.

Risk

Mitigation

The VCF 9.x installer enforces prerequisites as hard gates. DNS, NTP, NIC speed, disk eligibility, and IP availability are checked before anything deploys, and bring-up stops on failure.

Complete and verify DNS, NTP, and IP planning before the deployment window. Treat the planning workbook as a deliverable, not a formality.

The 9.1 management footprint is heavier than earlier releases. Topology choice (simple versus highly available) and component sizing drive host and memory requirements.

Size the management domain against the chosen topology and component set, not against a minimum host count.

Hardware is not fundable from the entitlement, but some pathways require it. A design can stall waiting on capital that was never approved.

Establish in discovery whether separate capital exists. If not, rule out greenfield and design around convergence and import.

Existing NSX deployments are not initially supported for convergence into a VCF management domain. A fresh NSX instance is installed as part of the process.

Identify NSX presence during discovery. It may force a different pathway for that estate.

Update-release support windows are shorter than the line-level EOGS date.

Inventory exact builds. Some clusters may already be unsupported.

Skills gap on the platform team, particularly NSX and Automation.

Fund enablement early in the program, not with leftover budget at the end.

Chargeback stalls on finance approval rather than technical delivery.

Scope it jointly with finance from the start, or deliver showback and stop there.

Entitlement expires partly unspent because the customer&apos;s team could not absorb the delivery rate the plan assumed.

Model the required monthly burn rate in month one. Review monthly. Shift toward low customer time workstreams if actual falls behind plan.

Scope not captured at SOW time cannot be added without a change order through the prime, and cross-partner change orders are slow.

Scope the full twelve months, depth tiers included, before signature. Treat Sections 4 through 8 as a pre-sale exercise.

A deliverable we can describe has no matching Broadcom service definition and cannot be priced.

Map every intended deliverable to a service definition during scoping. Raise gaps with the engagement manager early.

Escalation has no direct contractual path to the customer sponsor because we are not the prime.

Agree the escalation route in writing at kickoff and keep the prime&apos;s engagement manager close.

Consultant availability is not booked against the engagement through the prime&apos;s demand process.

Secure and book resourcing when the plan is agreed. Late requests compete with every other partner.

Scope is padded late in the program to consume a remaining balance.

Agree the full twelve month scope in Q1 with depth tiers named. Raise a shortfall honestly and early while a scope or timeline change is still possible.

Our own consultant availability is not secured across the full twelve months.

Book delivery capacity at the same time the plan is agreed, not sprint by sprint.
